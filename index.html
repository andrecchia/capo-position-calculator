How this capo calculator works. A capo shortens the strings and raises the pitch of the whole guitar. This tool works backwards: starting from the chords you actually want to hear, it checks every capo position from fret 1 to 9 and figures out which "shape" — the chord you physically finger — produces that sound. It then ranks the options, favoring shapes you can play as open chords (C, D, E, G, A, Am, Em, Dm) over ones that still require a barre, and giving extra weight to capo positions within the first five frets, which stay easiest to play and keep the guitar's tone bright. You can also lower a whole progression by a number of semitones first — useful when transposing a song to a singer's range — before finding the matching capo shapes.
Do it by hand (no tool needed)

When you don't want to play a chord because it needs a barre, a capo lets you play an easier shape somewhere else on the neck and get the same sound. Working out where to put the capo takes two pieces of information.

First, the 12 notes in order: C, C#/Db, D, D#/Eb, E, F, F#/Gb, G, G#/Ab, A, A#/Bb, B, then it repeats. Moving 1 step in this list is the same as moving 1 fret on the guitar.

Second, only 8 chord shapes are truly easy to play, meaning no barre is needed: C, D, E, G, A for major chords, and Am, Em, Dm for minor chords. Every other chord, in its normal position, needs a barre.

With those two facts, the process is:

  1. Take any chord from your song — the first one is a good place to start. Say a song needs the chords Bb, Cm, Eb, Gm, F: the first chord is Bb.
  2. Check whether it's major or minor, then look at the easy shapes of the same type. For each one, count how many steps up the note list it takes to reach your chord. Bb is major, and the nearest easy major shape is A, just 1 step below it.
  3. Try the smallest count first. Subtract that same number of steps from every other chord in the song, and check how many of them also land on an easy shape. Trying capo fret 1 — subtracting 1 step from each chord — gives A, Bm, D, F#m, E. Two of those, Bm and F#m, still need a barre, so this isn't a good fit.
  4. If most or all of them land on easy shapes, that's your capo position. If too many still need a barre, go back and try the next-smallest count instead. Going back to Bb, the next-nearest easy shape is G, 3 steps below it. Trying capo fret 3 — subtracting 3 steps from each chord — gives G, Am, C, Em, D. All five are easy shapes, so that's the answer: capo on fret 3, playing G, Am, C, Em, and D.
